How to Write a UX Portfolio Case Study

How long a recruiter looks
Your main points in view10sec
One case study~1min
A whole portfolio(Balázs, 2026)3–5min

Roughly what a recruiter hiring design interns gives a single case study. In that window, your layout gets read before your sentences do.

You get about a minute. That's how long a recruiter usually spends on a case study before deciding whether to keep going, so this guide pulls apart four real student case studies to show what those readers look for and how to put it right where they'll see it.

Written forDesign students applying to internships and writing their first case study.

1

Step 1: Where case studies live, and who reads them

A portfolio case study is part product design writeup, part personal branding, part job application. It has one job. Prove you can take a project from a fuzzy problem to a result someone can measure. Polished screens can't do that on their own, so the case study has to show how and why you made your decisions along the way.

  1. Resume or applicationYour portfolio link goes on a resume, LinkedIn, a social post, or an application form.
  2. Portfolio siteUsually Framer, Webflow, Figma Sites, or hand-coded. The reader clicks one project on your work page.
  3. Recruiter skimAbout a minute of scanning headings, images and numbers. You win or lose them right here.
  4. InterviewYou'll talk through this same case study out loud, so write it knowing you'll have to say it.

Who reads it

  • Recruiters, screening a pile of candidates for quick signals.
  • Hiring managers, who want to see your process and how you made calls.
  • Design peers. They read slower, mostly to see how you presented the work.

How they read it

  • Fast, on a screen, comparing you against lots of other candidates.
  • In a top-to-bottom skim. They slow down only when something catches their eye.
  • With no way to ask you what you meant.

Who writes it, and when

Mostly university students and early-career designers, writing after a project wraps up (or, let's be real, while it's still going). Most student projects are team efforts. That's the tricky part: you have to pull your contribution out of the group's work and make it obvious.

The page has to make the whole argument. Someone opens your link on their own time, and you aren't there. Any reason or result you leave off the page simply doesn't exist for them.

2

Step 2: Anatomy of a case study

Read enough case studies and you'll see they're built from the same parts, in close to the same order. All four samples here follow it. Each part below is shown in the Cattlelog case study. Tap the yellow pins on a screenshot to see why that part works.

  1. 1Header block
  2. 2Problem statement
  3. 3Research & pain points
  4. 4Solution & features
  5. 5Numerical evidence
  6. 6Challenges & reflection
  7. 7Call to action
1

Header block

The first screen answers what a recruiter wants to know before scrolling at all. What is this? What did you do? How long did it take, and who else was on it?

Include

Project name, a one-line summary, your role, timeline, tools, and team.

Cattlelog skips a Tools line. Mellow has one (Figma, Notion). If the job post names specific tools, add yours.
AOne line of subtitle explains the product: "Streamlining Course & Professor Discovery." For some skimmers, that's the only sentence they'll read.
BRole, timeline and team are stacked in a tidy little column. "2 PM, 1 PD, 5 SWE, 1 PMM" quietly tells the reader the author was the team's only product designer.
CThe finished product is on screen immediately. Lead with the result, then tell the story of how you got there.
2

Problem statement

One or two sentences: who's struggling, what's missing, and why it matters. Everything else on the page should trace back to this.

Formula
[Users] don't have [what they need] to [reach a goal], so they [current workaround].
AThe statement gets its own shaded box, with the key gap picked out in blue. Your eye lands on it first, which is the whole point.
BA sticky section menu rides along as you scroll. A recruiter who only cares about "The Solution" or "Reflection" can go straight there.
3

Research & pain points

How do you know the problem is real? Say which methods you used (interviews, surveys, competitive analysis, affinity mapping), then what they turned up.

Keep it short

Nobody wants your raw notes. Boil the findings down to 3 to 5 named pain points.

A01, 02, 03. Numbering turns the research into chunks you can count at a glance.
BEvery pain point is a bold headline plus one sentence. Skimmers read the headlines; careful readers get the detail too.
4

Solution & features

Now show the design, and tie every feature back to a pain point you named earlier. Iterations belong here as well: wireframes, high-fidelity screens, and the options you threw out.

Pattern

Feature name → one sentence on what it does → screenshot.

AFeatures get a number and a plain label ("02 Grade Distributions"), the same pattern the pain points used.
BSeveral screens are layered into one image. Between them they answer the "lack of professor information" pain point, and nobody had to write a long paragraph to do it.
5

Numerical evidence

A number proves impact faster than any adjective can. Think users, drop-offs, time saved, sign-ups, test results.

Didn't launch? You still have numbers. Count the surveys you ran, the interviews you held, the screens you cut, and the testers who finished the task.
AEach metric sits in its own pill in big type, with one highlighted. Even mid-scroll, they're hard to miss.
6

Challenges & reflection

Something went wrong. Something always does. Say what it was and what you did about it, because it shows you as someone who iterates and learns, and recruiters say that's what they look for (Balázs, 2026).

Write each challenge as

Problem → what you did → result.

AA short name ("Visual Clutter"), then the explanation. It reuses the pain point card style, so the page feels like one piece.
B"Decreased this click-off rate by 80%." Drop a number into the story and a reflection becomes evidence.
7

Call to action

Don't leave the reader at a dead end. Tell them what to do next, whether that's getting in touch or looking at more of your work. All four samples close with an email, a LinkedIn link, or a "Next project" link.

Cattlelog has contact links at the bottom but no "Next project →". Mellow has both, and signs off with "Let's work together," which is about as upfront about job hunting as you can get.
AThe email address is spelled out. A recruiter can copy it straight away instead of wrestling with a contact form.
BLinkedIn and GitHub give recruiters somewhere else to look. The same links show up across the rest of the site.

Want to see every note at once?

Why this order? Two conventions happen to line up. The sections track the design process itself (problem, research, iteration, testing, reflection). And when recruiters describe the story they want, it's nearly the same one: problem, approach, process, result (Boza, 2026), or goal, journey, challenge, resolution (Balázs, 2026).

Seen in the other samples

Mellow, FindMyLab and ThreadIt use the same conventions, just not always in the same form. These have pins too.

Step 3: Six strategies for writing one well

Knowing the parts gets you halfway. The rest comes down to six decisions, pulled from the four samples and from what recruiters say they want. Get them right and people actually read your case study instead of skimming past it.

Define your role and team

Role, timeline, tools and team go in the header block. After that, "I" statements do the work of separating what you did from what the group did. All four samples handle this, each a bit differently. Ayson writes the whole page in the first person. Salas names teammates and mentors and gives them credit.

Do

"I designed the landing page, which cut click-off by 80%."

Don't

"We built a great product." On a team project, that leaves the reader guessing which part was yours.

Design for a 10-second read

Your problem, your role, and your best result should be the first things a skimming recruiter sees. Menus, bold phrases, numbered lists and short chunks all make it easy to jump around (Balázs, 2026; Boza, 2026).

Do

Lean on headings, numbers and whitespace until the headings and images could tell the story by themselves.

Don't

Write walls of text. Nobody gets through a dense paragraph in a one-minute skim.

Show why, not just what

For every decision, say why. Include the options you rejected, since your reasoning is the only window a reader has into how you think. ThreadIt numbers each change to its onboarding flow and puts the reason right next to it, backed by Nielsen's heuristics.

Do

Lead with the problem and your thinking. Show the iterations, and say why each one changed.

Don't

Open with a tool list, or show only the polished final screen.

AThe toggle labels carry a number: 14 steps before, 8 after.
BEach change is numbered on the flow and explained beside it (Reframed, Removed, Reframed). What changed and why, in one glance.

Prove it with evidence

Claims need numbers behind them. Not every project ships, though, so pick the kind of proof yours can honestly offer. Each sample leans on a different kind:

SampleKind of proofWhat it shows
CattlelogGrowth2,000+ daily users, 55,000+ total users, and an 80% drop in click-off
ThreadItReasoningChanges backed by heuristics, 14 → 8 screens, time-to-value from 10 minutes to under 2
FindMyLabAdaptabilityA pivot after a founder's critique, and about 100 sign-ups from a spreadsheet directory
MellowResearch volume50+ surveys, 12 interviews, and two rounds of usability testing
Do

Quantify wherever you can, even if it's "4 of 5 testers finished without help."

Don't

Lean on adjectives like "intuitive" or "seamless." Every other candidate is saying the same thing.

Include challenges and lessons

A reflection section is where you show that you learn from what happens. When Balázs (2026) surveyed recruiters, they said they look for clear thinking, self-reflection, and evidence of learning. Most of the samples have a challenges or takeaways section.

Do

Name a real problem, what you did about it, and what changed.

Don't

Pretend everything went smoothly. That hides how you think.

Write for designers

Write in the first person, and keep it confident and plain. Your readers already speak the vocabulary, so don't stop to define it. Spend those words on your reasoning.

  • MVP
  • HMW
  • affinity map
  • FTUX
  • usability testing
  • time-to-value

State your role clearly, keep the layout scannable, and tell a four-part story: problem, approach, process, result.

Paraphrasing Boza (2026), a UX recruiter writing for Toptal

Show your reasoning, your iterations, and your results. That's what recruiters report looking for.

Paraphrasing Balázs (2026), based on a survey of 74 recruiters

Sidebar: how it compares to scholarly design cases

The portfolio case study has an academic cousin. Putting them side by side explains a lot about why portfolios look the way they do.

Scholarly design case

Written so others can learn

Boling (2010) calls the design case a specialized and critical form of design knowledge, and argues it should be a rigorous account of real design work.

Reader
Other designers and researchers
Goal
Help others learn from the project
Shows
Problem, decisions, outcomes, constraints

Portfolio case study

Written so a recruiter can judge you

Same core: a real problem, the decisions, the outcomes. It's just been reshaped for the job search.

Reader
Recruiters and hiring managers
Goal
Show the author's judgment and skill
Shows
Your role, headline metrics, polish

That difference in purpose is why portfolio case studies put your role and metrics up front. Rejected options and constraints still show up, but in a portfolio their job is to show off the writer's judgment.
