When a mistake happens in an organization, those responsible for the person(s) behind the mistake have the duty to do whatever it takes to ensure the mistake can be resolved and won’t reoccur in the future. Workers tell their managers, managers tell their managers, and the cycle repeats for a seemingly endless count of different roles.

This hierarchy exists because of the fallibility of humans. The inevitability of imperfection is the definition of humanity. And at the bottom of the chain, this hierarchy is obvious. Managers tell their workers to fix their issues.

But, as we extrapolate this pattern further up the chain of command, we eventually hit a limit. There must be someone at the head of the chain. And when the inevitable catches up to them, who tells the leaders they are wrong?

---

Let us analyze this situation, starting from the base workers. If a worker is stupendous at their current position, it would be evident that they should be promoted up the chain of command to expand their responsibilities to someone who has proved themselves in the past.

Again, we extrapolate upwards up the chain of command over time. However this time, we have a different limit: incompetency. Each person can only be capable at their role till a certain extent. And if a worker is incompetent in their current role, they are not qualified to be promoted, having not proved their value. By this standard, we can then interpret every individual who is not the leader to be someone who has been promoted until they are incompetent.

However, this point has no relevancy to whether the leader themselves is competent. In fact, we should assume the leader themselves is part of this chain due to the human nature of incompetency.

---

Having shown all individuals in an organization are incompetent to some extent, we can again attack the issue: Who tells the leaders they are wrong?

In reality, the answer should be, well, everyone.

Once again, humans are incompetent creatures. There is no such thing as a perfect human. Thus, it is the responsibility of all individuals, not just those in charge, to promote the collective intelligence and decision making.

Accuracy disregards seniority.

The biggest (yet most common) mistake anyone in this chain can make is assuming omnipotence. But again, incompetency in the realm of humility is another humanistic trait. Thus, we end at a new issue: How to tell the leaders they are wrong.
