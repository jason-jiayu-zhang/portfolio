Let us begin by defining the two ideas at hand. I define alignment as a consensus amongst a group towards a goal, and agreement as a consensus amongst a group of people towards an action.

For a group of people to be aligned, they must fundamentally agree on the root cause, purpose, or goal that the efforts of the group are being directed towards. The specifics of that root are irrelevant. It then must be that an aligned group as made agreements towards actions they are carrying out to reach their root. And this is what aligned groups appear to be to externals of the group. But external appearance and internal workflows are not the same despite how often they are conflated.

Samuel Goldwyn, the co-founder of Goldwyn Pictures, has a wonderful quote that encapsulates how internal team dynamics should function:

> “I don’t want any yes-men around me. I want everybody to tell me the truth, even if it costs them their jobs.”

> YES-MAN [ˈyesˌman] “a weak person who always agrees with their superior”)

Fundamentally, a person who never disagrees with anything provides no new insights or content related to anything they work on. A person who only regurgitates what is presented to them provides no additional value beyond what they are handed. This is the most useless person on a team, regardless of their output or throughput. Why? A lack of conflict.

But isn’t conflict bad?

To most, conflict is bad. But this is due to the circumstances in which conflict usually arises: when someone [redacted] up. If conflict only ever appears when someone is to fault, there must be consequences for the fault that arise in the ensuing conflict. This is the cause of this misconception.

The semantics of the word conflict imply some negative connotation. Let us focus now on the denotive definition of the word:

> CONFLICT [ˈkɒnflɪkt] “a state of mind in which a person experiences a clash of opposites.”

Let us now add an additional layer between alignment and agreement. I define conflict as a divergence amongst a group towards execution. Given a group of different people with different worldviews and different experiences, it is impossible that they view every problem through the same lens, if even ever at all. These variances in ideas and ideals requires the presence of conflict to resolve differences.

With this interpolation, we can now frame our new definition of agreement as a consensus amongst a group of people towards the result of a conflict.

So which one kills organizations?

It isn’t conflict. It’s agreement without alignment, and by extension, alignment without conflict.

A team that strives towards pure agreement discourages conflicts, creating an environment of passive compliance. They collect “yes-men” who only nod along while internally disagreeing with the root goal. The organization moves, but in every direction at the same time, leaking momentum to an invisible friction not visible through any sort of metrics.

Demand that everyone follows the Why, debates the How, and as a result—not as a cause—agrees on the What.
